# WorldCast Live: the multiplayer web demo

A web page where several people play one recorded round start together. Each player's view is generated by their own
WorldCast client on their own GPU, from their keyboard, gamepad or touch screen; the clients share only player state and scene
state, as in the paper. The lobby shows a top-down overview of each map: click a point and the room starts at the
round start nearest to it, with you in the seat there ([Start from a map](#start-from-a-map)); rooms can also be
opened from a list and joined by link. The play view shows the generated view at its own 672 x 384 pixels, with
input-to-photon latency, network quality, the key caps and the players around it, and a spectator view shows every
player's view at once.

Everything is in `worldcast/demo/`; `tools/serve_demo.py` starts it. Without a GPU, workers run a mock client that plays
pre-rendered frames and fakes the state model, so the whole system runs on a laptop.

## Quick start

Pick the line that matches your machine; each serves the page at <http://localhost:8100>.

**1. A laptop, no GPU** (the mock client, a synthetic arena, nothing to download):

```bash
pip install -e ".[demo]"
python tools/serve_demo.py --role local --workers 2
```

Open the address in two browser windows, start a room in each and take a seat. `--role local` serves the coordinator
and its workers from one process, the frames through the coordinator's port, so another device on the network can join
through the same port. `--config configs/demo/mock_block16.yaml` switches the mock to the paper's 16-frame blocks.

**2. One GPU, or a few on one machine** (the real model: one player per card, shown at 16 fps). It needs a library of
recorded round starts, `demo-library/library.json`, built with `tools/build_demo_library.py` ([Library](#library)), and
`data/paths.yaml` ([docs/inference.md](inference.md#data)):

```bash
pip install -e ".[demo]"            # plus the flash-attn wheel for your CUDA (docs/installation.md)
python tools/setup_demo.py --library demo-library/library.json --gpus 0,1
python tools/serve_demo.py --role all --gpus 0,1 --config configs/demo/worldcast.yaml \
    --set library=demo-library/library.json
```

`setup_demo.py` downloads the release weights and the tiny decoder into `weights/`, checks that the library loads and
that `data/paths.yaml` names your dataset files, and prints the serve command.
`--role all` starts the coordinator here and one worker process per card in `--gpus` (`0,1,2`: three workers; `0+1`: one
worker that spans two cards, for the original Wan2.2 VAE on a second card, [below](#the-original-vae-on-a-second-card)).
Stopping it with Ctrl-C stops the workers. The GPU worker needs the library: its warm-up replays the library's first
seat, and the synthetic arena has none. Check the install with the mock client (`--role local`).

**3. Several machines** (the coordinator on a CPU host, a worker per GPU anywhere): [Run on GPUs, across
machines](#run-on-gpus-across-machines).

The library of round starts, the overview images and how to build them are in [Library](#library) and [Start from a
map](#start-from-a-map).

## Playing

**Controls.** The pause panel lists them and has the settings; press Play to start.

- **Keyboard.** `W A S D` move; the arrow keys turn (left and right the yaw, up and down the pitch) at a constant rate,
  120 degrees a second by default and 0.8 of that for the pitch (the panel's Turn speed, 20 to 320); `Space` jumps, `C` or
  `Ctrl` crouches (on a Mac, `Ctrl` with an arrow key is the system's, so crouch with `C` while you turn), `Shift` runs (walks, with Walk by default off; see below),
  `1`-`4` pick weapons, `Q` the last weapon, `R` reloads, `F` inspects with a knife (the generator learned the inspect
  of a gun badly: with another weapon `F` does nothing, and its key cap and line of the legend are greyed; the worker
  clears the button from every frame that holds no knife too), `Esc` pauses; `M` marks on the view where the
  model places the other players (the field's markers). While you play, the page takes these keys and the mouse's middle,
  back and forward buttons from the browser (`Ctrl+1`-`4` would switch tabs, back would leave the view and drop your seat).
  A window that loses the focus stands still with its view uncovered (the windows of a room are its players': click the
  one you play). On a Mac, `Cmd` lets go of every key (macOS sends no key release while it is down), and a key pressed
  with `Cmd` held does nothing.
- **Mouse.** On a desktop browser the mouse turns the view: Play captures it (the pointer lock), `Esc` frees it and a
  click on the view captures it again; the panel's Mouse look switch turns that off, and its Mouse slider sets the
  sensitivity (0.03 degrees a pixel by default). With Mouse look off the cursor stays visible and the mouse only
  fires. A browser that cannot lock the pointer (Safari on an iPad) has Mouse look off, whatever was set, and its switch
  disabled. A browser may refuse to capture the mouse (Chrome does for about a second after `Esc`): the page says so and
  you play on with the keyboard until you click the view, which asks again. The left and right buttons fire and
  alt-fire when the view is clicked.
- **Gamepad** (the browser's standard mapping; a browser shows a pad after a button is pressed, and some offer pads only
  to an https page or localhost). The right stick looks: nothing within 12% of its travel, from 25 degrees a second just
  beyond that to the Max turn speed at full deflection. The left stick moves, in eight directions (a direction counts beyond 40% of its
  travel). A jumps, B crouches, X reloads, Y inspects (with a knife), the right trigger fires, the left trigger alt-fires, the bumpers
  take the weapon before and after the one held, the D-pad's up, right, down and left take weapons 1-4. On Linux many
  pads come with no standard mapping; the page knows Microsoft's Xbox 360, One and Series pads (the xpad driver) and Sony's
  DualShock 4 and DualSense by the vendor and product the browser names, says "connected (Xbox layout)" or
  "(PlayStation layout)", and plays them the same way (a PlayStation pad's Cross, Circle, Square and Triangle are the
  A, B, X and Y places). Any other pad with no standard mapping is said not to be used.
- **Touch** (a phone or a tablet: a coarse pointer). An on-screen pad outside the view: a left stick that moves and a
  right stick that looks (the gamepad's curve), jump and crouch above the left stick, fire and the next weapon above the
  right one, all usable at once. A stick works from where the thumb lands: it turns or moves only as the thumb moves
  from there. Upright it is below the view, on its side at the view's sides. Tap Play to start; the
  pause button in the HUD pauses.

**How the view turns.** As in the game: the mouse turns the view as far as it moves, however far and fast (a flick
of 180 degrees is a flick of 180 degrees), and the page sends its turn with the next input sample; only the first
movement after the pointer lock takes hold is dropped, which may be the pointer's jump to the lock. The arrow keys and
the sticks turn at a rate, at most the panel's Max turn speed: 240 degrees a second of yaw by default (30 to 320) and 0.8
of that of pitch (a touch device has no settings: 240). The generator was trained on a turn of up to 20 degrees a frame,
so the worker does a turn in as many frames as keep each within that, and what a block's frames cannot hold goes on
into the next block's ([Latency](#latency)); the pitch stops at the camera's 89 degrees either way. Every frame is shown
as it was generated; the instant turn (below, off by default) turns it at once by the turn it does not hold yet, as far
as what the view has seen reaches, nothing mirrored, blurred, stretched or black at its edges.

**Instant turn.** The generated frames hold a turn a few tenths of a second after it is made ([Latency](#latency)), so the
page turns the view itself at the next animation frame (`static/turn.js`, WebGL2): a turn about the eye needs no depth,
so each pixel of the view at the orientation you look at now shows what a frame saw along the same ray, the newest frame
(taken at its header's `tn` and `cp`, the turn made, which the generator's view follows with the yaw it is fed
`play.yaw_gain` times: 1.45 with refresh, as the demo config runs, 1 without) or a recent one. The frames shown are
kept in a panorama over every direction (8 texels a degree, each written at its own orientation over the older ones),
and a frame of it is used for 8 seconds (`PANORAMA_AGE_S`) and while every frame since was taken within 16 units of
where you are (`PANORAMA_RADIUS_U`: moving changes the parallax, which a turn cannot show).
Where neither the newest frame nor the panorama has seen a pixel, the view turns only as far as the seen pixels reach,
and the rest of the turn shows when the frames that hold it arrive: a turn back to where you looked shows at once, half a
turn included, and a turn into what nobody has seen waits for the frames. A view turned between the orientations of the
frames it is drawn from lacks a sliver at its edges (a frame's edges are great circles, and the frames' pitches differ a
little), which it hides by magnifying the turned view by up to 3% (`MAX_MAGNIFY`), eased out as the frames catch up. The
weapon and the hands stay where the newest frame has them (`VIEWMODEL`: the right hand's piece from the bottom right
corner, the left hand's at the bottom left; their pixels never go into the panorama): a pixel of those pieces is drawn as
the newest frame has it, but for one that matches what the panorama saw there beside the viewmodel from another
orientation, which is the scene and turns with the view. While the viewmodel may be away from its pieces (a knife
carried at a run, a shot, a reload, a weapon drawn: the buttons and the weapon of the frame's row of the controls, its
header's `b` and `w`), the panorama takes only the band of the frame above where it reaches (`VIEWMODEL_TOP`), and
during a knife's inspect none of it, so that no turn shows a weapon where it was. A frame that holds the whole turn is
drawn as it was generated, and moving is not shown ahead. The panel's Instant turn switch turns it on (off by default,
the view turning only with the generated frames; kept as `wc.instantturn`); a browser without WebGL2 draws the frames
as they are.

**Walk by default.** The generator renders the player at the recorded gait, a run (about 250 units a second); the
walk button (`speed`) makes it walk at about half that. With the panel's Walk by default on (the default) the page holds
the walk button while a movement key (`W A S D`; an arrow key turns and does not move you) or the left stick is held, and
`Shift` lets go of it, so that you run while you hold it: the page's sample carries the button with the movement key and
changes with `Shift` at once. `Shift` alone holds nothing. With it off, `Shift` holds the walk button and a movement key
runs. The key cap of `Shift` lights for the key, the pause panel's legend and the cap's tooltip say what it
does, and the setting is kept as `wc.walk`. A gamepad or a touch pad has no run key, so it walks while the setting is on
(a touch device has no panel to turn it off, and a pad's left stick holds a direction past 40% of its travel, at one
speed).

**Motion feedback.** A movement key reaches the generator at the next block and shows in its frames after that, so
the view answers it before they do: from the press until the first frame that holds it is drawn, forward scales the view
up about its centre (by 4% at most), back scales it down (by 3%) and A or D shifts it against the move (by 2.5% of its
width), and keys held together add. The change rises along an ease-out to its cap over 0.4 s, falls to nothing over
0.2 s when that frame is drawn (or when the key is let go before it: a stop is not answered), and is one ramp for a
held key, so it never builds up; the generated frames show the move from there on. The panel's Motion feedback sets
how much of it: Off (the default) draws the frames as they are, Light the changes above, Strong twice them. A scaled
frame is a little blurred, and a band that the shift or a zoom-out leaves at the view's edge shows the frame mirrored
about that edge. Turning is not touched. The setting is kept as `wc.motion`; a touch device has it off and no panel to
change it. The input to photon of the HUD still counts to the first generated frame that folds the input in.

The settings are kept in the browser (`wc.arrowturn`, `wc.turncap`, `wc.mouselook`, `wc.sens`, `wc.instantturn`, `wc.motion` and `wc.walk` in localStorage). The
view is shown at the generated frame's own size, centred, never stretched; nothing is over it but the crosshair and the
markers, and the HUD is around it. While paused, click the frame rate and latency above the
view to open the latency breakdown, which also says how your own position is known: at the right of the view, in the
loadout's place, in a window 1260 pixels wide or more, where it stays while you play and moves nothing; in a narrower
one a popover under them that takes the pause panel's place and goes when you play (a click away from it closes it).
A window that crosses between the two closes it.

**Browsers.** A current Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari on Linux, Windows or macOS plays with the keyboard, the mouse and
a gamepad, and Chrome or Safari on a phone or tablet with the on-screen pad; the page asks the browser what it can do,
never who it is. It needs WebSockets, ES modules, `createImageBitmap` and a 2D canvas, and uses the rest where it is
there; a browser that keeps nothing in storage plays and forgets the settings; pointer lock serves the mouse look and the Gamepad API the gamepad (a page served by http from a host other
than localhost may not get the second). Keys are bound by position, so on an AZERTY keyboard `W A S D` are the keys
that lie there, Z Q S D. `Ctrl+W` is the browser's: crouch with `C`, and a tab that closes while you play asks first.
A laptop with a touch screen and a mouse or a trackpad plays with the keyboard; a device whose only pointer is coarse
gets the on-screen pad. Windows and Linux scrollbars, device pixel ratios from 1 to 2 and windows from a phone's to an
ultrawide's keep the layout whole; the page follows the system's reduced-motion, reduced-transparency and forced-colours
settings. Its text is set in the fonts it ships (Inter, Inter Tight, JetBrains Mono); the arrows of the key caps come
from the system's. The screen stays on while the view is open (where the browser has a wake lock). On a phone, Add to Home Screen opens it
full screen.

## Architecture

```
 browsers                         coordinator (1, CPU)                       GPU workers (1 per GPU, 1 player each)
 ────────                         ────────────────────                       ──────────────────────────────────────
 lobby page ── HTTP /api ───────▶ rooms, seats, library        ◀── WS ───── worker A: client (models loaded once)
            ◀─ WS /ws/lobby ────  room clock, worker registry  ─── assign ─▶   input buffer ─▶ step ─▶ JPEG
                                  relay of the clients'        ◀─ messages ─   its client's messages, as made
                                  messages: player states,     ─── relay ──▶ worker B, C ...
                                  memory entries (both kept
                                  for late joiners),
                                  withdrawals
 play view ═ WS /ws/play ══════════════════════════════════════════════════▶ worker A  (inputs in, frames out)
 spectator ═ WS /ws/watch ═════════════════════════════════════════════════▶ every worker of the room
```

**Coordinator** (`worldcast/demo/coordinator.py`, one process, no GPU). Serves the page and the lobby API, keeps the
rooms (a round start from the library, the seats) and the registry of workers. A join takes a free worker and assigns
it the seat; the browser then talks to that worker directly. Per room it relays what the clients exchange and nothing
else: their messages (player states, memory entries, withdrawals). For a player who joins late it keeps the newest
`scene_state.bound` memory entries of each player (the bound B of a live session) and twice as many of its live states
(the rows its camera used, one per block; `LIVE_STATES_PER_ENTRY`), and sends the joiner the live states first: the
joiner draws the others at once and keys each kept entry at the cameras it was generated with. A client admits another's
entry only once its own clock, its play time, has passed the entry's block, and a client's world state keeps the newest
max(2 B, 6) blocks of each player: a joiner admits the earlier players' entries only while it joins fewer than 2 B - 1
blocks behind them (B = 32: about a minute), and from a lead of 2 B - 1 blocks on the entries kept for it expire unused
(it still draws those players, and uses the entries of everyone who plays at its time or later). It drops them when the
player leaves. It owns the room clock, to which workers synchronise (NTP-style pings).

**Worker** (`worldcast/demo/worker.py`, one per GPU). Loads its client once and serves one player at a time. Per block
it cuts the player's inputs into the block's controls (`worldcast/demo/inputs.py`), steps the client and streams each
frame to the player (and to spectators) as soon as it is decoded, JPEG-encoded on its client thread; each browser
socket sends from a queue of its own ([flow control](#latency)). It sends the client's messages to the coordinator as
soon as the client queues them, in order, and when a session ends the client's last ones (the WorldCast client's
`done`) before `left`, which frees the seat; the other clients' messages go to its client as they arrive. The room's
roster tells the client who left (`remove_player`), including who left while it started; a player who took a seat
tells it itself, with its first live state. A block's cut more than `play.release_after_s` = 1 s after the browser's
newest input sample (a hidden page, a lost link) releases the buttons it held; the gaps of a congested link are
shorter, so a held key is not dropped for a whole block.

A session has no length: no block is the last (the client's round slides, [Live player states](inference.md#live-player-states)),
and it ends when its player leaves (`bye`), its browser stays away longer than `worker.reconnect_grace_s` (8 s), the
coordinator releases the seat, the coordinator link or the client fails, or the worker shuts down. A block that yields no
frame is the client's failure, not the round's end: the session ends with "the client failed". One more thing ends it,
which is not a limit on play: a seat that nobody plays, since a hidden page still acknowledges every frame and one
forgotten tab would hold a GPU for ever. The page tells the worker when it is hidden (`{"t": "vis", "h": true}` on
`visibilitychange`, and with every new socket), and the worker ends the session with "you were away" once the page has
been hidden for `play.away_s` (900 s) or has sent no key, button or turn for `play.idle_s` (900 s); any input, or
the page shown again, starts both over, so a player who plays is never released.

- *Pacing* (`worldcast/demo/pacing.py`). A block starts just in time: its first frame should reach the browser when the
  previous block's frames run out, so inputs are taken as late as possible and a fast client does not run ahead of the
  display. A block's critical delay counts from when its controls are taken, so a wait for the client to finish
  preparing the block does not count. The start time is the 90th percentile of the critical delays of the last 16
  blocks (`LEAD_PERCENTILE`, rounded down to one of them), plus 40 ms of margin (`PACING_MARGIN_S`): one slow block
  does not make the blocks after it start early.
- *Warm-up.* Before it registers with the coordinator, and before it listens for browsers and `/health`, a worker warms
  its client up (`warm_up`). The WorldCast client generates a replay session of the library's first seat on its
  recorded player states and controls until each window shape, 17 and 21 latent frames, has run (`WINDOW_SHAPES`), for
  at most 16 window blocks (`WARM_UP_WINDOWS`) or to the round's end; its messages are dropped. What the first
  player's blocks would otherwise wait for happens there: the generator compiles each of its calls and captures its
  CUDA graph when the call recurs. The six blocks of latents 1-24 make every call a window makes, at window positions
  1-17 and five times each, and the first window block makes the first frame's again: the generator is warm after
  seven blocks, whichever window shapes ran. The session also runs the decoder and the scene state's writes and
  retrievals. The worker logs the blocks and window shapes it ran, and warns of a shape that did not run; a worker
  whose library's first seat cannot be replayed stops with the error.
- *Latest messages.* A client steps on the other clients' messages that have arrived, so one slow player does not
  stall the others: the paper's deployment. Its evaluation runs the clients in lockstep (`tools/run_session.py`).
- *Display rate.* The worker paces for a display of `play.fps` = 16 frames per second, the recording's rate, so a
  block's 16 frames last 1 s, which an H200 keeps up with; an H20 takes 1.067 s a block in a long session and needs
  `play.fps` = 15 ([Latency](#latency)). The browser follows the rate the blocks actually arrive at, and drops the
  oldest frames when it falls behind by more than a few.

**Client** (`worldcast/demo/clients.py`, `ClientProtocol`), the seam between a worker and the model:

| call | meaning |
|---|---|
| `warm_up()` | once, before the worker registers: make the first session's blocks as fast as the later ones (the WorldCast client's replay session); nothing of it is sent |
| `start(round_start, seat)` | join the room's session as the seat's player, at the round start (models stay loaded); the room's other players are in the world from their first live state |
| `step(controls)` | generate the next block: call `controls()` once, right before its denoising, and yield the block's frames (`LiveFrame`) as they are decoded, each with the players as the client's player state field read them (`LivePlayerState`: the player's own row and those of the other players it has in the world, each with where it comes from and whether the field draws it), the field's markers and how the own position is known |
| `blocks_in_flight` | an attribute: 1, the worker takes a block's frames before it steps the next; 2 (the WorldCast client whose decoder has a `decoder_device`), it steps the next block while the frames of the last are still being decoded, and `step` then only requests its block |
| `can_refresh`, `refresh(controls, switch, generated, preview_step)` | with `play.refresh`: whether the newest block can be generated again, and its generation again from newer controls (`generated()` once its latents are ready), yielding its frames from the first one `switch` says has not been sent (with `preview_step`, first the frames of that denoising step's x0, `LiveFrame.preview`); a refresh generated while the block's frames are decoded may take the decode over, and `step`'s iterator then yields its frames ([refresh](#latency)) |
| `refresh_cards`, `refresh_card_free`, `card_freed` | the cards a refresh may run on (1; the WorldCast client's `refresh_devices`), whether one is free now, and a callback the worker sets, called when one becomes free ([refresh on more cards](#refresh-on-more-cards)) |
| `stop()` | leave the session; the messages it queues then (the WorldCast client's `done`) go after all the others |
| `take_messages()` / `receive_message(message)` | the clients' messages (`ClientMessage`), opaque to the network; a live state (kind `live`) and a memory entry (kind `scene`) are kept for late joiners |
| `notify` | set by the worker: called on the client's thread whenever a message is queued, so that it goes at once |
| `remove_player(slot)` | another player left: hidden from the next block on, whether or not its own last message arrives |

A seat is what a person takes in a room: one player of the round start, by its player slot in the recorded round
(`slot`, 0-9). `worldcast.demo` exports these names; a client of another model implements `ClientProtocol`.

`MockClient` (`worldcast/demo/mock.py`) implements it without a GPU: it sleeps `denoise_ms` per block and `decode_ms`
per frame, plays a seat's pre-rendered clip (or draws the arena), integrates the controls into a position (on open
ground, without bounds), and publishes memory entries of the real size (774 kB), so the relay carries the real
traffic. It shows the other players as the WorldCast client does under `client.mock.player_state_source`. With `live` (the
default) the camera follows the keys and each block's rows leave at its controls cut as its live state; another player
is drawn at its rows, from its first live state until it leaves, and a seat nobody plays is nowhere. With `gt` the
players, each on the spawn of its seat, are drawn into the camera of the player's recording, its spawn, while the keys
move the view: every other player stays on one spot of the frame as the player walks, the drift that live play
removes, there to compare with. `WorldCastClient`
(`worldcast/demo/worldcast_client.py`) adapts `worldcast.engine.inference.client.Client`
([GPU integration](#gpu-integration)). A room's workers run one kind of client.

**Browser** (`worldcast/demo/static/`, plain ES modules, no build step): `app.js` (router, lobby, seat picker),
`play.js` (the play view and its HUD), `input.js` (keyboard, mouse, gamepad and touch as input samples), `pad.js` (the
gamepad and the on-screen pad), `latency.js` (the input-to-photon accounting), `stream.js` (WebSocket, JPEG decode with `createImageBitmap`, a playout that shows the
frames at the rate the blocks arrive at, [Latency](#latency)), `watch.js`
(spectators). The page takes what it shares with the server from the server: the button order of an input sample, the
weapons that inspect and its play settings from the worker's `welcome`; the players' colours, the name length and the
frame's pixel size from `GET /api/lobby`. The play view reads a frame's header for the clock (`s`, the room time of the player's own state,
shown as `h:mm:ss`: a session has no end), the own position's status (`st`, for the latency breakdown), the
field's markers (`m`) and which players the field draws (`drawn` in the rows of `p`); it draws no map of the players.

**The play view** (`play.js`, `app.css`). The stage is the generated frame at its own size, `frame_pixels` of `GET
/api/lobby` (672 x 384 CSS pixels), centred in a bezel, and never larger. Every window of 1000 x 700 pixels or more
shows it whole, and so does a laptop's short one (1366 x 650: the margins above and below the frame are slimmer in a
window under 700 pixels high); a window too small for the frame and its surroundings shrinks it in the frame's aspect
ratio: one shorter than about 620 pixels, one narrower than 740, or one narrower than 1000 and too short for the cards
below the key caps (about 790 pixels: their row is 126 pixels high, and the frame does not move as players come and go).
The canvas holds the frame's pixels whatever the screen (`stream.js`: a frame stretched into a larger store, by the
device pixel ratio or the window's size, is only blurrier), and while the stage
is the frame's size on a screen of a whole number of device pixels per CSS pixel the browser shows each pixel of the
view as a square, unsmoothed. Over the stage while one plays: the crosshair and the field's markers (their canvas has
the stage's rectangle, in the screen's pixels up to two per CSS pixel), nothing else. A marker is a ring at the player's
feet with its label above, an outline of the player's colour on a dark one: its radius is 4000 over the depth, 30 pixels
at most (60 across, the figure's width close up) and at least 12 (24 across, 14 high) however far the player is, so it
shows at any distance. Around the stage: above, who plays, the clock, and the frame rate and latency; below, the size of
the frame, the latest notice and the key caps; on its left the players of the room, and on its right the loadout and,
once opened, the latency breakdown in its place. The two cards take the width of their column, 264 pixels at most: under
190 pixels they drop the seat tag and the team, and in a window of 1000 to 1259 pixels they are narrow cards at the
sides; one narrower than 1000 puts them below the key caps. While the player is alone in the room the players' card says
that others appear in the view when they take a seat. The breakdown shows how the own position is known (`st`), with
what it means in a sentence: `guarded` (the keys' physics, corrected by the model's reading of where the player is,
which is checked first), `reacquiring` (a reading that disagreed for a while is being followed, a little each frame) or
`degraded` (the reading keeps disagreeing and is ignored, the position follows the player's movement as far as the view
followed it); it is a diagnosis for the breakdown, no word on the bar. A phone or a portrait window puts the view on top
at the full width, up to 672 pixels, and the HUD under it. A device whose pointer is coarse (a phone, a tablet) shows
the on-screen pad outside the view, never over it (`pad.js`): upright, at the bottom of the window under the HUD and
the players, where the thumbs are, and in view however many players the room has; on its side, at the view's two sides,
with the view shrinking only as far as the window needs (whole on a tablet, smaller on a phone),
the latest notice taking the HUD's place for its moment. The crosshair stays; the key caps, the loadout and the
breakdown do not (the weapon button says which weapon is held). A pause button in the HUD opens the pause panel, which
for a touch device says how the pad plays and has no keyboard settings. The pause and start panels cover the whole page,
blurred, and are gone while one plays; the pause panel lists the controls and has the settings (Mouse look, the mouse's
sensitivity, Turn speed, Max turn speed, Walk by default, Instant turn, Motion feedback), sits under the frame rate and latency (over them where the window has more room there; in a
window with room on neither side the panel takes the whole window and they go under it), and scrolls its list in a
window too low for it, its Play button staying in view. Otherwise the frame rate and latency, and the breakdown they
open, stay above the pause panel, for the mouse is free only then. The bezel, its
brackets and rulers and the grid behind them are decoration: CSS, static, nothing the page measures

**The lobby and the seat picker** (`app.js`, `app.css`). The lobby has the round starts as tiles, standing on one line

## Protocol

HTTP (coordinator): `GET /api/lobby` (round starts, rooms, workers, the page's constants), `POST /api/rooms`
`{round_start}`, `GET /api/rooms/<id>`, `POST /api/rooms/<id>/join` `{slot, name}` (returns the player's
`play_url`), `GET /api/maps` (per map its images, floors and seats), `POST /api/start` `{map, u, v, floor, name}`
(the nearest seat; the join answer), `GET /library/<file>`; the page, `/` and its files under `/static/`, revalidated
on every load, so that a browser never runs a page older than the workers it talks to. WebSockets: `/ws/lobby`,
`/ws/worker` (workers), `/ws/proxy/<worker>/<play|watch>` (`frame_route: proxy`). Workers: `/ws/play?token=`,
`/ws/watch?room=`, `/health`.

Text messages are JSON `{"t": type, ...}`; binary messages are `u32 header length | JSON header | payload`
(`worldcast/demo/wire.py`).

| direction | message |
|---|---|
| browser → worker | `in` {`q` sequence, `b` held buttons, `dp` `dy` degrees turned since the last sample, `w` weapon id}, an input sample: at the display's frames, at most 60 a second (every frame on a display of 60 Hz or less), and at once on every key or button change; `ack` {`r` frame}, a frame received, sent as each frame arrives (play and watch sockets); `markers` {`on`}, whether the player's page draws the field's markers (play socket); `vis` {`h`}, whether the page is hidden, sent on `visibilitychange` and with every new socket (play socket); `ping`; `bye` |
| worker → browser | `welcome` {seat, roster, weapon ids, `buttons`, `fps`, `frames_per_block`, `jitter_buffer_frames`, `margin_frames`, `inspect_weapons`, `backlog_frames`, `mouse_degrees_per_pixel`, `tn` [pitch, yaw] degrees turned in the session's input samples before this socket}, `roster`, `pong`, `ended`; binary frames (a frame may come again, a newer version, with `play.swap_sent`: it takes the place of the one not yet shown if it is newer): header {`k` frame, `pv` whether it is a refresh's preview, `v` the version of its block it is of, `r` (1) on a frame sent again, `q` newest input sample in the frame, `tm` [input sample → controls taken, controls taken → frame ready, encode] ms, `s` own state, `p` the states of the other players the client has in the world, `st` how the own position is known, `tn` [pitch, yaw] degrees turned in the session's input samples through the frame, `cp` the camera's pitch at the frame, `b` and `w` the buttons held and the weapon in the frame's row of the controls, `f` whether it is its block's first, `m` the field's markers while the page asks for them}, payload JPEG |
| worker → coordinator | `hello`, `assigned`, `left` (after the client's last message), `clock`; binary: header {`room`, `slot`, `kind`, `meta`}, payload: a client's message, sent as the client queues it |
| coordinator → worker | `assign` {room, round start, slot, token, room clock origin, roster}, `release`, `roster`, `clock`; the relayed client messages (to a joiner first the kept live states, then the kept memory entries) |

The page drops frames at a block's first frame (`f`) by the welcome's `backlog_frames` ([Latency](#latency)).

A player state on the wire is `[slot, room time, x, y, z, yaw, pitch, alive, vx, vy, src, drawn]` (engine units,
degrees and units/s; yaw counter-clockwise from +x, pitch > 0 looks down): the row the client's player state field
read at the frame's latent frame (`Client.field_rows`), the row of the latent frame's last video frame, so the four
video frames of a latent frame carry the same rows, each at its own room time. `src` says where it comes from, a code
of `FieldRows` (`OWN` 0, `LIVE` 1, `RECORDED` 2): the player's own client, another client that plays it live, or, in
the mock client's `gt` mode, the seat's recording; `drawn` is 1 where the field draws the player at the latent frame
(its visibility label at the block's first denoising step), 0 where it does not, `null` for the own row and the first
frame. A marker is `[slot, u, v, depth]`: where the field puts a player's feet at the latent frame, `u` and `v`
fractions of the frame's width and height from its top left corner, and its depth in u, for every other player in
front of the camera. `st` says how the own row is known: the status of the own position's estimate
([docs/inference.md](inference.md#live-player-states)), or the source the mock client stands in for. `cp` is the pitch
of the client's own camera at the frame itself (`Client.camera_pitch`), where `s` has it at the latent frame's last
video frame. (The mock client's rows and markers are those of each video frame.)

Bit `i` of `b` is button `i` of the `welcome`'s `buttons`, `CONTROL_BUTTONS` (`worldcast/data/controls.py`); `dp` and
`dy` are the degrees turned since the previous sample, whatever turned the view: a mouse move asks for
`play.mouse_degrees_per_pixel` degrees per pixel, all of it in the next sample, an arrow key or a stick its rate over
the time between display frames, at most the Max turn speed ([Controls](#playing)); on a display faster than
60 Hz a sample carries the turn of the frames since the one before, so that the turn sent is the turn made, whatever the
display. The worker folds a block's input samples into the model's
per-frame controls (`InputBuffer`): 11 buttons, the turn (raw, and mu-law encoded, `noclip`), the weapon id and each
frame's room time. `play.input_mapping: latest` (default) holds the buttons held at the cut for every frame, adds the
buttons tapped since the previous block to its first frame and spreads the turn over the block, so an input shows from
the block's first frame on; `front` does the same with the turn spread over the block's first latent frame (four
frames), or over as many more as keep each frame's turn within the generator's trained 20 degrees; `hybrid`
chooses between the two for each block by the turn it holds, `front` for a block that turns less than 20 degrees (the
larger of its pitch and yaw) and `latest` for one that turns that much or more, and feeds the generator the block's yaw
1.1 times ([Latency](#latency)); `realtime` puts each sample on the frame shown while it was taken, at about one
more block of latency. A cut more than `play.release_after_s` after the newest sample holds no button. Each sample's
turn is folded into some frame, so a frame's `tn` counts the turn of every sample up to its row of the controls: the
turn its view holds, but for any pitch turn the pitch limit cut, and the turn the player made whatever the gain on the
yaw the generator is fed. The round's recorded first frame holds none of its block's turn.

## Run on GPUs, across machines

One coordinator anywhere reachable by browsers and workers; one worker per GPU. Every host reads the same library
file; GPU workers also need the weights (`weights/paths.yaml`, with `weights/taew2_2.pth`) and the data paths
(`data/paths.yaml`, [docs/inference.md](inference.md#data)), which `configs/demo/worldcast.yaml` names. Run from the
repository root: relative paths resolve against the working directory.

```bash
# the coordinator (CPU host "lobby"), port 8100
python tools/serve_demo.py --role coordinator --set library=/data/demo-library/library.json

# one worker per GPU (GPU host "gpu-1"), ports 8101, 8102, ...
CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0 python tools/serve_demo.py --role worker --config configs/demo/worldcast.yaml \
    --set library=/data/demo-library/library.json \
    --set worker.coordinator_url=ws://lobby:8100 --set worker.port=8101 \
    --set worker.advertise_url=ws://gpu-1:8101
CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=1 python tools/serve_demo.py --role worker ... --set worker.port=8102 \
    --set worker.advertise_url=ws://gpu-1:8102
```

Browsers open `http://lobby:8100`. By default (`coordinator.frame_route: direct`) they open each worker's WebSocket
at `worker.advertise_url` (or the address the worker connected from). When browsers cannot reach the GPU hosts, set
`--set coordinator.frame_route=proxy` on the coordinator: frames and inputs then pass through it, one hop more. Behind
HTTPS, put the coordinator behind a TLS proxy and use `proxy`.

## Config

The defaults are those of `worldcast/demo/config.py`. Each `--config` file merges over them in order, `--set
key=value` overrides one dotted key (YAML-typed), and `--print-config` prints the config the role runs with (for
`--role local` and `--role all`: with `frame_route: proxy`; the role itself gives each of its workers a loopback
address).

| section | read by | keys |
|---|---|---|
| `library` | both | `library.json` of the round starts; `null` = the synthetic arena |
| `scene_state` | both | `bound` (32): B, the own memory entries a client keeps and the coordinator keeps per player |
| `coordinator` | the coordinator | `host`, `port`, `frame_route` (`direct` / `proxy`), `max_rooms`, `empty_room_s` |
| `worker` | a worker | `host`, `port`, `coordinator_url`, `advertise_url`, `worker_id`, `jpeg_quality` (85, the player's frames while its link keeps up), `max_inflight_ms` (250, how long a browser's link may take to deliver a frame behind those it has not yet acknowledged, at least two of its round trips; [Latency](#latency)), `reconnect_grace_s` |
| `client` | a worker | `kind` (`mock` / `worldcast`); `mock`: `frames_per_block`, `denoise_ms`, `decode_ms`, `jitter_ms`, `player_state_source` (`live` / `gt`, the `player_state.source` it stands in for); `worldcast`: `configs` (the inference YAMLs), `overrides` (dotted keys of them; `player_state.source: live`, `live.idle_context: freeze`, `live.idle_with_peers: true`, `live.wall_keys: edge`, `walls` or `guard`, `live.dead_zone_u: 4` and `live.hop: spread` where wanted), `taehv_path` (the tiny decoder), `serving` (fields of `ServingOptions.low_latency` to replace, e.g. `decoder: wan` and `decoder_device: 'cuda:1'`, [the original VAE on a second card](#the-original-vae-on-a-second-card), or `refresh_devices: [cuda:0, cuda:1]`, [refresh on more cards](#refresh-on-more-cards)) |
| `play` | a worker (the page takes them from its `welcome`) | `fps` (16, the frames shown per second), `jitter_buffer_frames` (1, the frames the page holds before it shows the first), `margin_frames` (1.5, the frame periods before it is due that the page's playout has every coming frame arrive: less is less latency and more holds), `input_mapping` (`latest` / `hybrid` / `front` / `realtime`; by default `front` with `refresh` and `latest` without), `yaw_gain` and `pitch_gain` (the generator is fed these times the player's yaw and pitch, the camera and the page keep the turn made; by default 1.45 on the yaw with `refresh`, which makes its view turn 0.9 of a flick and 1.15 of a steady turn, else 1), `mouse_degrees_per_pixel` (0.03, the page's mouse sensitivity until the player sets one), `release_after_s` (1.0, a cut this long after the newest input sample releases the held buttons), `away_s` and `idle_s` (900 each: a page hidden, or a player without a key, button or turn, this long loses its seat; any input starts them over), `refresh` (false; true: the block on screen is generated again when the input changes, [refresh](#latency)), `refresh_threshold_deg` (1.0, the turn since the block's last cut or refresh that asks for a refresh), `send_lead_ms` (40, with refresh how long before the pacing's display shows it a frame goes), `gather_ms` (20, with refresh how long a refresh for a turn that starts waits for more of it), `preview_step` (0; 1-3: with refresh the frames of that denoising step's x0 go as the refresh's preview until its own take their places), `swap_sent` (false; true: with refresh a newer version of a frame that went goes too while the page has not shown it, and the page shows it in its place) |

A `--set` of a mapping replaces the mapping: to change two inference keys, give both in one
`client.worldcast.overrides`, with the six keys `configs/demo/worldcast.yaml` sets there: `player_state.source: live`,
`live.idle_context: freeze`, `live.idle_with_peers: true`, `live.wall_keys: guard`, `live.dead_zone_u: 4.0` and
`live.hop: spread`.

`run.latent_frames` is, in live play, the length of the round the client keeps, not of the session: the WorldCast client
sets 29 (the first frame, the first six blocks and one more, `LIVE_ROUND_LATENTS`; 45 for [the generator trained
without scene state](#the-generator-trained-without-scene-state), `live_round_latents`) unless its `overrides` give it,
and a longer round, given there, generates the same blocks. Its warm-up replays a round of its own length (the first six
blocks and `WARM_UP_WINDOWS` window blocks, so that every window shape runs).

## Library

A library is a `library.json` next to its images: its `round_starts`, each with a map, a start frame (source frame,
32 fps), the recorded round (`match_id`, `round`), the slot of the cover seat and the seats (`slot`, `team`,
`spawn` [x, y, z, yaw, pitch], `loadout`, `preview`, and for the mock client `clip`, a directory of JPEG frames). The
WorldCast client needs round starts of a recorded round whose start frame has a cached first latent, usually frame 0,
and, for a seat, 7 s of its recording after the start frame (the round of 29 latent frames: nothing of the recording
is read after the first frame, so the session itself is as long as its player plays).

Live play shows only the players a human plays, and the first frame of a seat's recording, which every window of a
block attends, would show the recorded players in it as ghosts: the field says nobody is there. So a seat of a recorded
round is offered only if no other player may be seen in its first frame, by the dataset's GT visibility labels at the
round start (the manifest's `visibility_label_root`, the data paths' `paths.visibility_label_root`). A player counts if
its label says it is visible, or if it is inside the seat's view frustum while its label is unknown (it may well be in
the frame); a player behind the camera, or labelled occluded, does not. A dead player is in no frustum, so a corpse in
the first frame is not detected: a round start at the start of the round (`video_start: 0`) has none. The builder logs
how many seats each round start offers and each seat it leaves out, with the players or the weapons, leaves out a round
start none of whose seats qualifies, makes the first seat that remains the cover when the cover was left out, and
refuses a manifest that leaves no round start (the tool then ends with a message and status 2). A seat without a
recording has no labels and is offered as it is.

Live play does not track the scope either: the client marks the scope unknown while a weapon that zooms is held
(`worldcast.data.camera.SCOPE_ZOOM_FOV`: AWP, SSG 08, SCAR-20, G3SG1, AUG, SG 553), the field of view stays unscoped,
and the generator, which saw a scoped view only with the scope known, draws a black scope view from the first frame.
So a seat whose loadout holds such a weapon is left out of the library too, whether the loadout comes from the seat's
tick table (the primary weapon it held longest) or from the manifest, spelled as the model reads it (`AWP` and
`weapon_awp` are the AWP), and the log says which weapon, by the model's name (`nuke-r03, seat 5 left out: its loadout
holds awp, which zooms (live play does not track the scope)`). A round start whose seats all hold one is left out like
one whose seats all see a player. The seats that remain play every weapon of their loadout.

The rule thins a round start: at the start of the two Nuke rounds of the test data (rounds 15 and 17), 2 and 3 of the 10
seats qualify, and the others see a player at once. Give the manifest several round starts and read the log for what
each offers. `WorldCastClient.start` applies both rules when a seat is taken and refuses a seat whose loadout holds a
weapon that zooms, or whose first frame may show another player, naming the weapons or the players, so a library built
by hand, before the rules or against other labels cannot bring a black scope view or those players into the world.

`python tools/build_demo_library.py --manifest library.yaml --out-dir demo-library` builds one from a short manifest
(its format is in `worldcast/demo/build_library.py`'s docstring): per round the rendered video of every seat; with the
data paths, each seat's team, spawn pose and loadout from the round's tick tables.

## Start from a map

The lobby draws each map of the library as a top-down overview. Moving the pointer marks the starting spot nearest to it
(a blue dot with a tick for the heading); a click anywhere on the map starts a room there and seats the player: the page posts the click to `POST /api/start` (`{map, u, v, floor, name}`, the click as fractions of the
image), the coordinator takes the nearest seat of the map (of the floor shown, on a map with two), opens the room,
seats the player and answers with the same join answer as a room link. When every GPU is busy it answers 503 and leaves
no room behind. `GET /api/maps` is what the page reads: per map its images, floors and spots.

A spot is a seat of a round start of the [library](#library), so the library decides where a player can start: build it
with round starts spread over the map, and each map gets as many dots. A map with no overview image gets a plain panel
fitted to its spots, so the demo works without any image.

`overviews.json` beside `library.json` places the images on the world (the fields are in `worldcast/demo/map_picker.py`).
`tools/make_overviews.py` draws the images and writes it, from the navigation mesh of each map, which you export from your
own copy of the game's map files (the `navmesh_hull_0` mesh of `de_<map>_navigation.glb`, in engine units); `--out-dir`
is the directory of `library.json`. It needs trimesh (`pip install -e ".[train]"`, or `pip install trimesh`) and Pillow
(`.[demo]`):

```bash
python tools/make_overviews.py --out-dir demo-library --split de_nuke=-520 \
    --mesh de_mirage=meshes/de_mirage_navigation.glb --mesh de_nuke=meshes/de_nuke_navigation.glb
```

`--split MAP=Z` draws a map with two floors (Nuke) as two images on one grid, told apart at height `Z`. The images
derive from the game's map files, so WorldCast ships the tool and not the images.

## GPU integration

`WorldCastClient` (`worldcast/demo/worldcast_client.py`) maps `worldcast.engine.inference.client.Client`, the same block
loop as the offline client, onto `ClientProtocol`. It builds the `InferenceConfig` from `client.worldcast.configs` and
`overrides`, passes the block's controls (`LiveControls`: with each frame's turn and room time) as the callable the
client calls right before its denoising, and wraps the client's `MessagePart`s as `ClientMessage`s. Each frame carries
the rows the client's player state field read at the frame's latent frame (`Client.field_rows`, `field_states`), the
field's markers (`field_markers`: the field's own projection, `project_players`) and the own position's status.

- Play is live: `player_state.source: live` ([docs/inference.md](inference.md#live-player-states)), which
  `configs/demo/worldcast.yaml` sets. The client's camera follows the player's keys (the field's own row, the rays,
  retrieval, the keys of its memory entries), so a player who stands still stays where it stands while the player
  walks and turns. Only players a human plays are in the world: another player is drawn at the rows its own camera
  used (its `live` part, one per block, about 0.9 kB) from its first part on, a seat nobody plays is nowhere, and a
  player who leaves is hidden from the next block (`remove_player` hands the client a `done` of the seat, whether or
  not the leaver's own arrives). The first frame of a seat, which every window of a block attends, shows nobody
  ([Library](#library)). The own position is read off the generated video and guarded against jumps, a view that stood
  still and the map's edge (`GuardedPosition`, not the paper's Eq. (4)); its status is in the latency breakdown. The scope of a
  zooming weapon is not tracked: the field of view stays unscoped, and the library offers no seat whose loadout holds
  one ([Library](#library)).
- A player who is idle keeps a clear picture: `live.idle_context: freeze`, which `configs/demo/worldcast.yaml` sets
  (the inference config's own default is `none`). A block is idle while the player holds no key and turns the view by
  less than its controls resolve: about half a degree a block with `hybrid` or `front`, about two with `latest`, and
  about 0.12 degrees in one input sample with `realtime`. In a stretch of idle blocks the client keeps, for every block,
  the context of the stretch's first three, so the view it generates from itself stays what it was however long the
  player is idle; the frames shown are what each block generates. The first key or turn ends the stretch, and the blocks
  after it go on from that context. With `live.idle_with_peers: true` (also set there) other players in the room do not
  end it as long as none is drawn in the player's view: one who walks into view does, and a new stretch begins once none
  of the player's last four blocks shows another player. `live.idle_with_peers: false` ends it whenever another player
  is in the world over those blocks, and `live.idle_context: none` runs the block loop as it is
  ([docs/inference.md](inference.md#live-player-states)).
- A wall stops the player: `live.wall_keys: guard`, which `configs/demo/worldcast.yaml` sets (the inference config's
  own default is `held`, which keeps the keys; `edge` and `walls` are the other values), clears a block's movement keys
  from the row a wall the depth head sees stops its camera at, in the controls the client generates from, never at the
  edge of the map's walkable extent and for at most 3 blocks in a row, so that a player is not held. A refresh cuts its
  block again from the guard's state before the block's first cut, so the guard clears the keys of every version as it
  would the block's first ([docs/inference.md](inference.md#live-player-states)).
- `live.dead_zone_u: 4.0` and `live.hop: spread`, also set there (the defaults are 0 and `step`), keep another player
  where the picture has it when the viewer taps a key or moves: the first reads the state model's motion head for
  small steps too, the second lets the camera of a block start where the block before it ended and run into the
  position estimate ([docs/inference.md](inference.md#live-player-states)).
- The paper's sources are for offline sessions, and the client refuses them: under live keys `gt` keeps the camera on
  the seat's recording while the keys move the view, so the world drifts with the keys, and `predicted` runs the
  closed loop on the recorded controls. GT player states are the examples' videos (`examples/run.sh`, Table 3's
  setting); the closed loop is `tools/run_session.py` with `player_state.source: predicted` (Tables 2 and 4a).
- The client is served with `ServingOptions.low_latency` (CUDA graphs, `torch.compile`, the tiny decoder, no
  lockstep; [the original VAE on a second card](#the-original-vae-on-a-second-card) is a setting of it) and attends with FlashAttention-3 when it is installed (`model.attention: fa3`; else flash-attention 2,
  with a log line) ([Latency](#latency)). `--set 'client.worldcast.serving={compile: false}'` with
  `--set 'client.worldcast.overrides={model.attention: flash, player_state.source: live, live.idle_context: freeze, live.idle_with_peers: true, live.wall_keys: guard, live.dead_zone_u: 4.0, live.hop: spread}'`
  serves it with the exact client's kernels (a `--set` of the overrides replaces those of the file, the six keys
  included).
- The bound: a client keeps `scene_state.bound` = 32 own memory entries, not the paper's 64 ([Latency](#latency)).
  It is the demo's setting, applied to the inference config, and the coordinator keeps to it too.

## The demo's inference in evaluation form

`tools/replay_live.py` plays a recorded client through the live path (`player_state.source: live`): alone in its
world, on the recording's own controls, with the settings of the evaluation or, with `--demo`, of the demo's worker.
The first client of an example case, both ways, and the latents decoded as the evaluation decodes them:

```bash
python tools/replay_live.py --config weights/paths.yaml --config examples/data/mirage_r16/config.yaml \
    --index-row 0 --out-dir runs/replay/exact
python tools/replay_live.py --config weights/paths.yaml --config examples/data/mirage_r16/config.yaml \
    --index-row 0 --out-dir runs/replay/demo --demo configs/demo/worldcast.yaml
python tools/decode.py --config weights/paths.yaml --latents runs/replay/exact/latents.npy runs/replay/demo/latents.npy
```

Each run writes `latents.npy` and `replay.json`, which has the settings it ran with and those that differ from the
config. What is the evaluation's in both: the weights and the generator call, the four denoising steps, the context
noise and the order of the random draws, the entry noise (a seed gives a live client the noise of the paper's run),
the first frame and the first six blocks, the window of a block (21 latent frames, 17 where nothing is retrieved) with
its retrieval and memory entries, the recording's controls and their encoding, bf16 and TF32. A client that stands and
turns with nobody visible generates the evaluation's latents bit for bit, on a round that slides too
(`tests/engine/inference/test_live_equals_evaluation.py`). With `--demo` the worker's settings replace the
evaluation's: FlashAttention-3 and `torch.compile` ([GPU integration](#gpu-integration)), B = 32 and the idle context.

## Latency

The HUD's number is input to photon: from each key press, click, or the start of a turn (mouse, arrow key, stick), to
the moment the first frame that folds in that input is drawn, the median over the last 24 inputs. The breakdown splits it into sampling,
network, waiting for the next block, generating, encoding and the jitter buffer; the worker reports its parts per
frame, so no clock synchronisation is needed. With blocks of `F` frames shown at 16 fps and denoising of `L` ms, an
input waits on average half a block for the next block, then the denoising: input to photon ≈ `F × 62.5 / 2 + L +
buffer + network`. On a MacBook with the mock client and three players, everything the demo adds on top of the
client (sampling, network on a LAN, encoding, buffer) is about 100 ms.

**The display rate and the bound.** Three players of a Table-3 round (Dust2 round 9), one H20 each, the whole round:

| H20, three players | stalls per minute once the memory bank is full | input to photon, p50 |
|---|---|---|
| B = 64, 16 fps (the paper's bound, the recording's rate) | about 49 | 1.02 s |
| B = 32, 15 fps (what an H20 keeps up with: `play.fps` 15) | about 5 | 1.10 s |

With the paper's bound a block takes longer than its second of frames once the memory bank is full. With B = 32 at
15 fps an H20 gives up about 0.1 s of latency and plays about 6% slower than the recording for a display that keeps
up; 97% of the blocks still retrieve a memory entry.

**No time limit.** What a block costs does not depend on how many have been played: the round the client keeps is the
shortest the loader allows and slides, the memory bank is bounded by B, and what the world state, the scene state and the
state model's reader keep is bounded ([Live player states](inference.md#live-player-states)); what no block reads is
bounded too (a live client's `Client.records` hold its newest 8 blocks, the scene state's keys of entries and of step
records go with their withdrawal), but for a pointer a block in the list of known blocks (8 bytes) and the blocks
skipped of a session with other players. A live client decodes a frame from its block's `x0` rounded
to the generator's output type, which is what the output holds (the output's latent frames are written again as the
round slides), so the pixels are the same as when the output was decoded and a recorded run of the demo is a baseline
for a later one (minute 1 of a long session is the baseline of that session); the paper's clients decode the output.
Whether the picture stays good over many minutes, and what a block costs on a GPU host over an hour, are measured on the
GPU (`tools/bench_live.py` over a long session), not on the CPU.

**Session start, pacing and playout.** Three things keep the start of a session and a late block from costing
latency or a freeze:

- *The warm-up* ([Architecture](#architecture)). A worker's first session would otherwise compile the generator and
  capture its CUDA graphs in its first blocks: on four H200s its first frame came after 73 s, and input to photon was
  3.7-4.4 s at the median for the next 15 s, against 0.67-0.79 s in the later sessions of the same workers.
- *The pacing* (`worldcast/demo/pacing.py`). A block's critical delay counts from when its controls were taken, and
  the start follows the 90th percentile of the last 16 blocks: one slow block does not start the next 16 blocks early,
  and a slow first block paces only the block after it. The percentile leaves out the two slowest of the 16, so about
  one block in six needs more than the lead, which the 40 ms of margin and the browser's playout absorb. The block
  after a slow first block starts at once and comes early, while the display still holds the slow block's frames. The
  schedule's display drops what the page drops (below): a block that arrives while more than `BACKLOG_FRAMES` = 3 frame
  periods of the blocks before it wait is shown 3 frame periods after it arrived, so that the early block leaves no
  backlog in the schedule, from which every block after it would be timed. That display runs at the frame period, with
  no jitter margin and no display ticks, so its first-frame wait is the optimistic one: the page's is longer by about
  its margin (0.10 s where blocks come when the display needs them, the schedule's 0) and the same within a frame
  period for a block that comes early (`tests/demo/test_playout.py` runs both on the same blocks).

  The lead keeps the first blocks' delays for a while: the 2nd to the 6th block of a session, before scene state, take
  longer than the blocks from the 7th on, and the 16 blocks of the percentile hold them until the 21st block starts, so
  the blocks in between start earlier than they need. The page takes that up by playing faster, up to 1.25 times; in a
  steady session a block's first frame waits about 0.11 s in the page for the display.
- *The playout* (`stream.js`). The page measures the period at which the blocks arrive (frame `k` arrives a block
  period after frame `k - 16`) and shows each frame for the shortest period that still has every coming frame arrive
  a frame and a half (`play.margin_frames`, from the worker's welcome), or two jitters, before it is due, between 0.8 and 1.25 of the blocks' frame
  period. When the blocks come late, playback slows down instead of freezing and bursting; when frames wait beyond
  the margin, after a late block, it catches up within a few blocks. Each frame is due a period after the one before
  it was due, not after the animation frame that showed it, so the playout keeps its rate on a display of any refresh
  rate. One late block moves the measured period little (a block counts as at most two frame periods, or four
  jitters, off it), and a frame that arrives more than two block periods after the one a block before it ends a pause
  of the stream (a reconnect, a stalled GPU) and starts the measurement over. A frame still missing when due is held
  on screen and the next shows as soon as it arrives (the HUD counts these holds as stalls).

  That catching up is a fifth of a second per second at most (0.8 of the period), so a display further behind than that
  would carry its backlog for ten blocks or more. A display that falls behind is handled so: when a block's first frame
  arrives (`f` in its header), the page keeps the frames before it that its display shows in `backlog_frames` frame
  periods (`BACKLOG_FRAMES` = 3, 0.19 s at 16 fps, the worker's number in its `welcome`) and drops the older ones. A
  display that keeps up with the frame period (its measured interval within the 1.25 the playout stretches to, whatever
  the noise of the interval) keeps that many frames, a slower one fewer, down to none; the count steps down by one at
  1.25 frame periods, where a display keeps one frame more or fewer by the noise of its interval. The block's first
  frame then waits for the frames kept in front of it and for the display's next frame time: on a display that keeps up
  3 frame periods and up to one more, 0.19-0.25 s at 16 fps (0.10-0.11 s in a steady session, where nothing is
  dropped); on a display that shows a frame every 150 ms one kept frame and the next frame time (a display's interval
  is learned from the frames it shows, so the first block keeps 3). A page hidden for a while leaves the measured
  interval alone (a frame shown more than two block periods after the one before it is no interval) and drops the
  blocks that came meanwhile but the frames kept before each. Back, it plays the frames it kept and then the
  newest block, about a block old (0.8 s for the first block shown in the test), until the next block's first frame
  drops what is left: one block of stale video after a long pause.
  `tests/demo/test_playout.py` runs the page's playout in headless Chrome on a warm and a cold session, on a display
  that keeps up, on one that falls behind in its first seconds and on a hidden page, block by block, and its rule on a
  table of display intervals; it skips without Chrome or Chromium, and where Chrome fails to dump the page, as the
  repository's tests need no browser: `tests/demo/test_static.py` pins the page's rule in text and
  `tests/demo/test_pacing.py` the schedule's.

  In a simulation of the H20's timings, calibrated to the measured 49 stalls per minute at B = 64 and 16 fps (it
  under-predicts B = 32 at 15 fps: about 1 stall per minute against the measured 5), the pacing and the playout bring
  the stalls down from 53 to 1.9 per minute there and to a tenth at B = 32 and 15 fps, for 25-100 ms more input to
  photon; a client that keeps up (an H200) has no stalls either way, and about 35 ms more. A margin of one frame would
  take 30 ms off and hold eight times as often at B = 32 and 15 fps, and on an H200 too once a link delays whole blocks
  by tens of ms.

**Frames as generated.** The play view draws every frame as it was generated, on a canvas of the frame's own pixels:
a turn shows in the frames that hold it, which refresh brings to a few tenths of a second (below), and before them the
instant turn turns the view at once as far as the frames shown have seen ([Controls](#playing)); nothing
mirrors, blurs or stretches the view. Movement shows with the frames, but for the motion feedback, a setting that is
off by default. The HUD's input to photon counts to the first generated frame that folds an input in.

`play.input_mapping` sets how a block's turn reaches the generator: `latest` (the default without refresh), `hybrid`,
`front` (the default with refresh) or `realtime`.
`latest` spreads the turn over the block's sixteen frames; `front` puts it in the block's first latent frame (more frames
where a frame's turn would pass the trained range); `realtime` puts each sample on the frame shown while it was taken.
`hybrid`, for a mouse, cuts a block that turns less than 20 degrees (`HYBRID_DEGREES`, the larger of its pitch and yaw) as
`front` and one that turns 20 degrees or more as `latest`, so a small correction acts at once, and it feeds the generator 1.1 times the block's yaw (`HYBRID_YAW_GAIN`). The gain is on the view controls only: the turn that
the camera, the field's projection, the state model and a frame header's `tn` count is the one the player made, as are the
memory frames a later block reads. It is on the yaw alone; the pitch has none. A block's turn is all in its own block:
none is held back for a later one.

**Refresh.** An input otherwise waits for the next block's controls, half a block on average. With
`play.refresh: true` (the default is false) the worker generates the block on screen again when the player's input
changes, and the input shows in the frames of that block not yet sent. The client (`live.refresh: true`, which the
setting gives it; `client.worldcast.overrides` may not set it) cuts the block's controls again from the first frame
expected not yet sent (`InputBuffer.recut`: the rows on their way keep theirs, the later rows take the buttons held
now, the taps since the last cut and the turn they had plus the new turn, so that the block's turn is the turn of
every sample folded into it; with `front`, the default with refresh, that turn is done in the two rows from the first
one not yet sent, or in as many more as keep each within the trained 20 degrees as the generator is fed it (the yaw
1.45 times by default with refresh, `play.yaw_gain`), so a turn shows in the frames of the refresh instead of spreading
over the rest of the block; what the rows left cannot hold so, a large flick near the block's end, is carried to the
next block's cut, `InputBuffer.carry`, which does it first) and runs its four denoising steps again on the same prefilled context, the same entry
noise and the same draws of the re-noise (`LiveRollout.refresh`). The new frames take the places of those not yet sent,
decoded from the latent frame of the first of them on the decoder's state before it (the tiny decoder and the Wan2.2
VAE alike, on a second card too). The worker sends each frame shortly before its display shows it (`SendQueue`,
`play.send_lead_ms`, 40 ms, ahead of the pacing's display) instead of as it is decoded, so that a refresh can still
replace it.

A refresh starts when a button or the weapon changes, the turn since the block's last cut or refresh reaches
`play.refresh_threshold_deg` (1 degree), or the turn starts, stops or turns back on either axis (the sign of the newest
input sample's turn against the newest one cut), while no refresh is being generated (inputs that come meanwhile go with the
next one); a turn that starts or turns back waits 20 ms (`play.gather_ms`; 0 waits for nothing) for more of it as the mouse goes on, unless it has
turned 20 degrees (`GATHER_DEGREES`: a large flick goes at once), so that more of a flick goes into one refresh, and while its latents would be ready and its first frame decoded before the next block starts; its decode is
held to the latent frames that end by then (also with the decoder on a card of its own: a decode that runs on into the
next step slows its prepare down on the host), and the frames shown stay the version's before it from there on. The newest block is refreshed from as soon as its controls are cut: an input that comes while its first
denoising runs is cut into it right after, and the block is swapped whole when none of its frames has been sent. A
refresh generated while the frames of the version before are decoded takes the decode over at the next latent frame (a
block's first version hands over to it there, from its switch on but never past the first frame not yet decoded), so
the decoder spends no time on frames a newer version replaces; with the decoder on the generator's device a refresh's
denoising waits for the block's first latent frame decoded. A block that comes early (the first blocks of a session leave the pacing's lead long
for a while) drops the frames of the block before it that the display would show after it, as the page drops them
without refresh, so that no backlog holds the newest block back. With the setting the worker's warm-up also plays its
seat live through the first window blocks and refreshes them, and the worker loads its JPEG encoder before it serves.
The next block's step commits the newest version of
the block on screen: its latents become the block's (the context of the blocks after it, its memory entry, the state
model's read) and its rows leave as the block's `live` part, once, about a block later than without refresh, so the
other players' clients draw the player that much further from its newest rows. The step then prepares and cuts its own
block; the pacing counts a block's critical delay from the start of its step, so the block on screen is refreshed until
as late as the next one can start and still come in time. The first six blocks are not refreshed. With the setting off
none of this runs.

```bash
python tools/serve_demo.py --role worker --config configs/demo/worldcast.yaml --set play.refresh=true ...
```

Five settings trade a little more of something else for less latency; `play.preview_step` and `play.swap_sent` are
off by default, and `configs/demo/worldcast.yaml` turns `play.swap_sent` on:

- `play.preview_step: 1` (or 2, 3): a refresh also hands the x0 of that denoising step to the decoder as soon as it is
  there, and its frames, the refresh's preview (`pv` in a frame's header), take the places of those not yet sent until
  the refresh's own frames take theirs: the input shows a few denoising steps sooner, from a coarser estimate of the
  block. A preview frame that went stays on screen until the refresh's frames follow (or, with `play.swap_sent`, is
  replaced on the page).
- `play.swap_sent: true`: a newer version of a frame that went (a refresh's, or a refresh's own after its preview) goes
  again, at once and marked (`r`), while it would reach the page before the page shows the frame (`play.margin_frames`
  frame periods after the pacing's display, less the send lead, half a frame period and the time the player's socket
  takes to deliver a frame queued now), and the page shows it in place of the frame it holds if it is a newer version
  (`v`, `pv`; `Playout.add`), and drops it if it holds none; a refresh then replaces the frames from the first a newer
  version would still reach in time, not from the first not sent. It costs the bytes of the frames sent again.
- `play.send_lead_ms` (40): less lets a refresh replace frames nearer the display, at the risk of a frame late on a
  link that jitters more; `play.margin_frames` (1.5): less shows each frame sooner after it arrives, at the risk of
  holds; `play.gather_ms` (20): less starts a refresh for a flick sooner, with less of the flick in it.

```bash
python tools/serve_demo.py --role worker --config configs/demo/worldcast.yaml \
    --set play.preview_step=1 --set play.swap_sent=true --set play.send_lead_ms=20 ...
```

**What is exact.** Always on, and bit-identical to the paper's client (checked on the H20 for a whole session, and on
the CPU by `tests/engine/inference`): the generator call of `worldcast/engine/inference/fast.py` (host-side cache
indices, the text embedding and cross-attention keys computed once per prompt, the per-block inputs built once per
block instead of once per step), CUDA graphs (`cuda_graphs=True`), preparing the next block's scene state and prefill
before its controls arrive, and streaming the decode of each latent frame on its own CUDA stream (the Wan2.2 decoder
streamed one latent frame at a time gives the paper's frames byte for byte). `lockstep=False` uses the other clients'
messages that have arrived instead of waiting.

**What is not.** `decoder="taehv"` (taew2_2, 9.9M parameters: 3.3 ms per latent frame instead of 360; PSNR 26.1 dB (SSIM 0.765, LPIPS 0.156)
to the Wan2.2 frames, slightly softer texture) and `compile=True` with `model.attention: fa3` (prefill + denoising 421 + 392 ms
instead of 492 + 475; same sharpness and frame-to-frame change, but a different kernel changes the last bits of every
step, so the rollout drifts away from the exact one: PSNR to it 37.4 dB in the first second, then about 20 dB). Such
options are judged by the quality of their own frames, not by PSNR to the exact rollout.

**Three clients.** Three clients of one round on three H20s of one node, exchanging messages in memory, run at
14.9-15.1 fps on the latest messages. In lockstep, the evaluation's mode, each block also waits for the slowest
client: 14.7 fps.

**Bandwidth.** About 43 kB per JPEG frame at quality 85 (5.5 Mbit/s down per player); inputs are about 60 samples/s
of under 100 bytes, and acknowledgements 16 a second. Each memory entry is 774 kB, pushed to every other worker of the
room: with 10 players each worker receives about 56 Mbit/s and the coordinator sends about 0.6 Gbit/s, which a
datacenter network carries. A live state is about 0.9 kB per block and player; the field's markers add a few dozen
bytes to a frame's header while the page asks for them.

**Flow control.** A browser whose link is slower than its stream gets fewer, smaller frames, never an ever older
backlog, and it slows nothing else down.

- Each browser socket on a worker sends from a queue of its own (`BrowserSocket`): the block loop, the other browsers
  and the client's messages to the room never wait for it.
- The page acknowledges each frame as it arrives (`ack`), and the worker measures each socket's link by the
  acknowledgements: its rate (the bytes it delivered per second it had frames in flight, over the newest 16) and its
  round trip (the shortest time from a frame's send to its acknowledgement). A frame goes when the link would deliver
  it, behind the frames in flight, within `worker.max_inflight_ms` = 250 ms, or within two round trips if that is
  longer, so a link with a long round trip still carries the full frame rate. Until the first acknowledgement each
  frame is taken to need its display time (4 frames in flight at 16 fps). The frames beyond wait on the worker.
- When a block's first frame comes while frames of an earlier block still wait, the browser is behind. The player's
  socket drops those frames and goes on with the new block, whose first frame shows the newest inputs; a spectator's
  keeps the newest block of frames. Either way its JPEG quality steps down once, 85, 75, 60, 50 (about 43, 34, 26 and
  22 kB per frame of generated video): a block's quality is chosen before it is generated, so the block after a step
  down still has the higher quality, and its frames, if they wait too, do not step the quality down again. It steps
  back up after two blocks in a row during which the socket was idle a third of the time.
- The player's frames adapt to the player's socket alone. The spectators share one encode of their own, at the lowest
  spectator's quality (a spectator starts a step below the player), so a slow spectator lowers the other spectators'
  picture but never the player's. While anyone watches, each frame is thus encoded twice on the client thread before it is sent: about
  27 ms more per block on the H20's host (JPEG encode, in the table above), which the player's frames wait for too,
  and which the frame header's encode time (`tm`) includes.
- With `coordinator.frame_route: proxy` the acknowledgements pass through the coordinator as the frames do, so the
  same budget bounds what the proxy holds; it forwards inputs, acknowledgements and pings as they come and drops
  nothing.

On the CPU (`tests/demo/test_worker.py`: the mock client with blocks of 16 frames, frames of those sizes, both frame
routes), a player reading over a 3 Mbit/s link gets frames under 1.5 s old, at quality 50 in whole blocks, while its
client's memory entries reach the room within milliseconds; over a 4.6 Mbit/s link it steps down once and keeps
quality 75; with a spectator on a 3 Mbit/s link, the player keeps 15 fps at quality 85, its frames a few milliseconds
old. Over a link with a 0.35 s round trip and no limit on its rate, every frame goes at quality 85; only the first
block may step the quality down once (back up two blocks later), while the link is not yet measured.

## Refresh on more cards

With refresh a new input waits while a refresh of the block on screen is generated. A worker that sees more cards keeps
a replica of the generator on each (`refresh_devices` of `client.worldcast.serving`, the client's card first): every
block's context is prefilled on all of them in parallel, a refresh is cut on the client's card in the order it was
asked for and denoised on a card that is free (the replicas first), with the same entry noise and re-noise, and the
newest version done is the one shown; the block's first version, its commit and the next block's prepare stay on the
client's card. A refresh asked for while the block's first version is denoised runs beside it.

```bash
CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0,1 python tools/serve_demo.py --role worker --config configs/demo/worldcast.yaml \
    --config configs/demo/worldcast_two_cards.yaml ...
```

Each card holds the generator (10 GB in bf16), its KV cache and its CUDA graphs; each is named by its index, once, and
none is the decoder's. With the Wan2.2 VAE on a card of its own, name the others (`CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0,1,2`,
`decoder_device: 'cuda:1'`, `refresh_devices: ['cuda:0', 'cuda:2']`). The warm-up denoises each window block of its
replay again on every replica, and in its live part refreshes each window block on the cards in turn, the block's own
controls giving its first frames again: every check is in `Client.replicas_equal`, and if one finds a card that gives
other bits than the client's (other kernels), or that fails, the replicas are let go (`Client.disable_replicas`, with a
warning) and the worker refreshes on its own card alone.

## The original VAE on a second card

The demo decodes with the tiny decoder (`taew2_2`) on the generator's card. A worker that sees two GPUs can decode with
the original Wan2.2 VAE instead, on the second card, while the first generates the next block: the frames of the paper
(streamed one latent frame at a time, byte for byte), at the cost of a card per worker and a display rate the decoder
sustains. Use it to show the paper's pictures; keep the tiny decoder where the display rate or the number of GPUs
matters more.

One client of the Table-3 round on an H20-3E (`tools/bench_realtime.py`, the demo's settings, paced as a worker paces):

| | tiny decoder, one card (the default) | Wan2.2 VAE, second card | Wan2.2 VAE, second card, channels-last |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPUs per worker | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| display rate of the run (`play.fps`) | 15 | 11 | 15 |
| sustained rate | the display's (17.0 fps at most) | 11.2 fps | 15.0 fps (17.1 at most) |
| decode of a latent frame | 5.7 ms | 356 ms | 186 ms |
| decode of a block of 16 frames | 23 ms | 1424 ms | 744 ms |
| added to the first frame of a block | 7 ms | 358 ms | 186 ms |
| input to photon | 0.99 s | 1.54 s | 1.17 s |
| frames | to the Wan2.2 frames: PSNR 26.1 dB, SSIM 0.765, LPIPS 0.156 | the Wan2.2 frames; the second card's equal the generator card's byte for byte | to the default decode: PSNR 54.7 dB, SSIM 0.999, LPIPS 0.0002 |

The Wan2.2 VAE on the generator's card, a block's frames decoded before the next block starts, sustains 7.4 fps. The
decoder's temporal cache serialises its work, so the second card does not shorten the decode of a block; it takes it off
the generation's path. The sustained rate is that of the slower of the generation and the decode: 16 frames in 1424 ms is
11.2 frames per second, below the 16 of the default display, so with the default layout set `play.fps` to a rate the
decoder sustains; with the channels-last layout (below) 16 frames in 744 ms keep up with 15.

```bash
CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0,1 python tools/serve_demo.py --role worker --config configs/demo/worldcast.yaml \
    --config configs/demo/worldcast_wan_vae_channels_last.yaml --set play.fps=15 --set library=/data/demo-library/library.json \
    --set worker.coordinator_url=ws://lobby:8100 --set worker.port=8101 --set worker.advertise_url=ws://gpu-1:8101
```

`configs/demo/worldcast_wan_vae.yaml` is `client.worldcast.serving: {decoder: wan, decoder_device: 'cuda:1'}`; the
same on the command line is `--set 'client.worldcast.serving={decoder: wan, decoder_device: cuda:1}'`, and the
decoder's `taehv_path` is then not needed. The generator runs on the first visible GPU (`run.device`), the decoder's
weights live on `decoder_device`, and each latent frame is copied there when it is decoded; the frames come back to the
host for the JPEG. A worker with one GPU and `decoder: wan` and no `decoder_device` decodes on the generator's card and
starts the next block when the last one's frames are out.

A worker with a `decoder_device` starts the next block while the frames of the last are still being decoded, and never a
third: at most one block waits for the decoder. A decode slower than the display therefore plays at the decoder's
rate: the frames come in order, none dropped, the time from a block's controls to its first frame is longer by the wait
for the decoder and does not grow. The pacing starts each block after the blocks still in flight (`Pacing.next_start`).

**Channels-last decoder.** `decoder_channels_last: true` keeps the VAE's decoding convolutions in the channels-last
layout: a latent frame decodes 1.9 times faster, in other cuDNN kernels, so the pixels are not the paper's decoder's
byte for byte (PSNR 54.7 dB to them, at most 29 of 255 on 17 % of the values). It needs `decoder: wan` and is off by default; `configs/demo/worldcast_wan_vae_channels_last.yaml` sets it with the
second card, and on the command line it is `decoder_channels_last: true` in the `client.worldcast.serving` mapping.

`tools/bench_realtime.py` measures it, with the stages of a block (`dit_ms`, `decode_ms_per_block`,
`decode_ms_per_latent_frame`, `first_frame_adder_ms`, `block_period_ms`) and the sustained `fps`, as it does the tiny
decoder; `--decoder-channels-last` turns the layout on:

```bash
CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0,1 python tools/bench_realtime.py --config weights/paths.yaml \
    --config examples/data/mirage_r16/config.yaml --index-row 0 --blocks 24 --fps 11 \
    --decoder wan --decoder-device cuda:1 --out-dir runs/bench-wan
```

## The generator trained without scene state

`configs/demo/worldcast_noscene.yaml` serves the four-step generator trained without scene state (stage `4_noscene`,
`model.scene_state: false`; not the paper's client; not released: train `stage3_noscene.yaml` and `stage4_noscene.yaml`
first, [docs/training.md](training.md#commands)), to set beside the released generator's demo.
`tools/make_noscene_generator.py --checkpoint <the stage's generator> --out-dir weights` makes its client weight file (the
generator's weights in bf16; the weights of the ray and observer-signal embeddings and of the visibility probe, which
the stage did not run, are left out and reported) and `weights/noscene.yaml`, which the demo config lists among its
inference YAMLs:

```bash
CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0 python tools/serve_demo.py --role worker --config configs/demo/worldcast_noscene.yaml \
    --set worker.coordinator_url=ws://lobby:8100
python tools/bench_live.py --config weights/paths.yaml --config examples/data/mirage_r16/config.yaml \
    --config weights/noscene.yaml --index-row 0 --demo configs/demo/worldcast_noscene.yaml --script walk \
    --seconds 60 --out-dir runs/bench-live/noscene
```

What differs from the released generator is what that stage trained on. No memory bank, retrieval or memory entry: a
client publishes its `live` part, and players see each other as with scene state. Its turns are encoded `clip` (the
input path's codes clamped to [-1, 1]). Its context is written clean, the field on it too. A block from latent frame 25
on attends to the ordinary window of the stage's training, `WindowLayout.ordinary`: the first frame, every latent frame
before the block up to 36, and the target frames, at the positions of the ten-second training window (29, 33, 37 and
then always 41 latent frames, the target frames at 37-40), with the visibility labels of all of them; so the round a
client keeps is 45 latent frames and slides from the eleventh block on, and its idle clip is nine blocks. The prefill
writes 37 latent frames in ten ranges where the released generator writes 13 or 17 in four or five, and the denoising
steps attend to 41.
